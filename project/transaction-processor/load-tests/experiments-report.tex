\begin{systembox}
\textbf{System under test.} A distributed money-movement pipeline on AWS. A stateless Go API (Gin) records each transfer as \texttt{PENDING} in DynamoDB and enqueues it on Amazon SQS; an asynchronous worker fleet drains the queue and commits debit, credit, and status update in a single atomic \texttt{TransactWriteItems}. Both services run on ECS Fargate behind an ALB in \texttt{us-west-2}. Every experiment in this report ran against the deployed AWS stack --- no local DynamoDB, no mocked SQS, no substitutes.
\end{systembox}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{screenshots/arch-diagram.png}
\caption{Deployed stack. Experiments sweep API and worker task counts across \{1, 2, 4\} and flip the worker's \texttt{LOCKING\_MODE} between \texttt{optimistic} and \texttt{pessimistic} via Terraform. Every AWS call is authorized by \texttt{LabRole}.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Experimental framing.} The four experiments cover the system's performance and correctness tradeoffs end-to-end. Experiment~1 stresses a single hot account to measure contention cost and retry behavior. Experiment~2 verifies crash safety by killing a worker mid-commit. Experiment~3 compares optimistic and pessimistic concurrency control under identical contention. Experiment~4 sweeps replica count to test horizontal scaling.

\textbf{Correctness invariant.} The sum of balances across 101 seeded accounts must remain \$1{,}010{,}000 (100 numbered accounts + 1 hot account, each seeded \$10{,}000). It is re-verified after every experiment with a strongly-consistent DynamoDB \texttt{Scan}, tolerating at most \$0.01 of float64 rounding noise.

\textbf{Measurement.} Locust exports per-endpoint CSV statistics. The worker service emits six cumulative counters (\texttt{transfer\_attempts\_total}, \texttt{\_success\_total}, \texttt{\_conflicts\_total}, \texttt{\_retries\_exhausted\_total}, \texttt{\_failures\_total}, \texttt{\_lock\_acquire\_failed\_total}) to CloudWatch Logs every 30~seconds as JSON snapshots. Experiment runners diff the earliest and latest snapshot inside each test window to compute conflict and retry rates.

%==============================================================
\section{Experiment 1 --- Concurrent Transfer Storm}
%==============================================================

\begin{purposebox}
\textbf{Purpose / tradeoff.} We evaluated \textbf{optimistic concurrency under pure contention} against the cost of retry backoff. A hot sender account forces every concurrent transfer into a conditional-check conflict candidate; the question is how much tail latency and retry work that imposes at a load the single worker can still handle.
\end{purposebox}

\textbf{Method.} \texttt{HotAccountUser} workload (every transfer originates from \texttt{hot-account-001}), 50 virtual users, 5-minute headless run, 1 API task + 1 worker task, optimistic locking. Harness: \texttt{make cloud-test-load-hot} against the ALB URL from Terraform outputs.

\centering
\small
\caption{Experiment 1 aggregated Locust statistics}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Endpoint} & \textbf{Requests} & \textbf{Failures} & \textbf{Median} & \textbf{p95} & \textbf{p99} & \textbf{Req/s} \\
\midrule
\texttt{POST /transfer [hot]} & 34{,}550 & 0 & 290 ms & 530 ms & 640 ms & 115.6 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Balance verifier output after the run: \texttt{Expected total 1010000.00, Actual total 1010000.00, Difference 0.00 --- All balances correct.}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{screenshots/aws-sqs-messages-inflight.png}
\caption{SQS \texttt{ApproximateNumberOfMessagesNotVisible} over the run window. The rise matches worker dequeue rate; the drain matches run termination. AWS-side confirmation that the queue absorbed real backpressure rather than being a silent pipe.}
\end{figure}

\begin{analysisbox}
\textbf{Analysis.} The system sustained 115~req/s under pure contention on a single account with zero HTTP failures. The p95 of 530~ms is dominated by the fact that every transfer is a conditional-check conflict candidate; the optimistic path retries up to three times with exponential backoff (100/200/400~ms), and enough retries land before the budget expires that the failure count stays at zero. The queue absorbs the backpressure at no point did requests starve at the API.
\end{analysisbox}

\begin{limitbox}
\textbf{Limitations.} A single Locust process at 50 users cannot saturate the system; this experiment characterizes contention cost at a load the single worker can handle, not the breaking point. A multi-host Locust pool would be needed to find the true ceiling.
\end{limitbox}

%==============================================================
\section{Experiment 2 --- Worker Crash Recovery}
%==============================================================

\begin{purposebox}
\textbf{Purpose / tradeoff.} We evaluated the architectural choice to bundle debit, credit, and status update into a \textbf{single atomic \texttt{TransactWriteItems}} against the alternative of three separate writes that would require saga-style compensation under partial failure. The goal was to show that a worker killed mid-commit leaves no half-applied transfer behind.
\end{purposebox}

\textbf{Method.} \texttt{PRE\_COMMIT\_DELAY\_MS=8000} on the worker, submit one transfer of \$25 via the ALB, \texttt{aws ecs stop-task} against the running worker 4~seconds into the delay window, wait for ECS to relaunch the task, wait for SQS to redeliver (visibility timeout 60~s), poll for terminal status, verify balances.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Experiment 2 recovery trace}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
Transaction final status & COMPLETED \\
ECS task replacement time & $\sim$30 s \\
SQS visibility timeout & 60 s \\
End-to-end recovery time (kill $\rightarrow$ terminal status) & $\sim$90 s \\
Duplicate commits & 0 \\
Balance difference after recovery & \$0.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{analysisbox}
\textbf{Analysis.} Three mechanisms cooperated correctly without explicit coordination. The worker only deletes the SQS message after a successful commit, so a crashed worker leaves the message visible for redelivery. The commit is one atomic \texttt{TransactWriteItems}, so the partial-apply scenario is structurally impossible. The transaction-status update includes \texttt{ConditionExpression: status = PENDING}, so the redelivered message's commit is blocked if some other worker already completed it. The architecture's durability story rests on the atomic commit; splitting debit and credit into separate writes would require compensation logic to avoid lost money.
\end{analysisbox}

\begin{limitbox}
\textbf{Limitations.} This tests one specific crash point (mid-commit during the pre-commit delay). A harsher test would fuzz the kill signal across the entire processing lifecycle --- between dequeue and read, between read and commit, between commit and delete --- to verify safety at each.
\end{limitbox}

%==============================================================
\section{Experiment 3 --- Optimistic vs.\ Pessimistic Concurrency Control}
%==============================================================

\begin{purposebox}
\textbf{Purpose / tradeoff.} We compared \textbf{optimistic concurrency} (retry on conditional-check failure) against \textbf{pessimistic leasing} (acquire a lock before commit). Optimistic pays only when conflicts happen; pessimistic pays a fixed lease-acquire round-trip on every transfer but never retries. The question is which regime wins at what contention level.
\end{purposebox}

\textbf{Method.} 2 worker replicas, \texttt{HotAccountUser} workload, 2-minute runs at 25 and 50 users, both modes. \texttt{scripts/compare\_locking\_modes.sh} flips \texttt{LOCKING\_MODE} via \texttt{terraform apply -var}, waits for ECS stability, reseeds, runs Locust, scrapes worker counters from CloudWatch Logs, verifies balances.

\begin{analysisbox}
\textbf{Analysis.} At our contention levels the two modes are statistically tied on throughput (108--115~req/s). The bottleneck is the single-partition DynamoDB write rate on the hot account; both modes must serialize on that partition and both do. Divergence appears in the tail: at 25 users, pessimistic's p95 is 120~ms vs.\ optimistic's 130~ms because optimistic pays a 100~ms, then 200~ms, then 400~ms backoff on every conflict. At 50 users both plateau at p95 = 540~ms because queue depth dominates per-request differences. The conflict- and retry-rate charts confirm the underlying mechanism: optimistic shows non-trivial conflict and retry rates; pessimistic shows near-zero. A third piece of evidence comes from AWS itself --- DynamoDB \texttt{ConditionalCheckFailedRequests} spikes during optimistic runs and collapses during pessimistic runs, matching what the worker counters report.

\medskip
\textbf{Conclusion.} Optimistic is the right default for low-contention workloads (it saves one AWS round-trip per transfer). Pessimistic is a targeted tool for hot-key workloads where retry backoff would blow up the tail.
\end{analysisbox}

\begin{limitbox}
\textbf{Limitations.} Both modes hit the same partition ceiling here. A truly informative comparison at much higher contention would require sharding the hot account across \texttt{hot-account-001-\{0..N\}}. We also did not measure the per-transfer cost difference from pessimistic's extra DynamoDB write; at scale that is the other axis of the tradeoff.
\end{limitbox}

%==============================================================
\section{Experiment 4 --- Horizontal Scaling Verification}
%==============================================================

\begin{purposebox}
\textbf{Purpose / tradeoff.} We tested the architectural claim that the API and worker tiers \textbf{scale independently} because they are decoupled by SQS. Sweeping the replica count with a fixed workload measures whether the design delivers that independence or silently serializes on some hidden component.
\end{purposebox}

\textbf{Method.} \texttt{TransferUser} mixed workload, 100 virtual users, 3-minute runs per configuration, optimistic mode. Replica pairs (api : worker) swept through \{1:1, 2:2, 4:4\} via \texttt{terraform apply -var api\_desired\_count=...}. Between configurations: ECS wait for stability, SQS purge, 65-second drain, account reseed. Harness: \texttt{scripts/scale\_experiment.sh}.

\begin{analysisbox}
\textbf{Analysis.} Two distinct regimes emerged. \textit{Regime~1 (1$\rightarrow$2):} throughput jumped 2.9$\times$ (84.5$\rightarrow$246.4~req/s), p95 collapsed 13$\times$ (1{,}600$\rightarrow$120~ms). A single worker at 100 users was queue-saturated; adding the second worker drained the queue fast enough that in-flight depth stayed near zero. \textit{Regime~2 (2$\rightarrow$4):} throughput flat at 247~req/s, p95 virtually unchanged. The bottleneck moved out of the worker fleet.

\medskip
\textbf{Conclusion.} The architecture scales near-ideally in the regime where the worker fleet is the bottleneck (the regime the SQS-decoupled design was built for) and degrades gracefully past that regime: no failures, no starvation, no correctness loss --- just flat throughput because no other tier is keeping up with the extra workers. Correctness held at every step: doubling and quadrupling replicas did not expose any new race condition or lost update.
\end{analysisbox}

\begin{limitbox}
\textbf{Limitations.} The 2$\rightarrow$4 flat region is evidence of \textit{some} ceiling but does not localize it. Three candidates remain: (a)~Locust's 100-user pool has reached its own think-time ceiling, (b)~the ALB is fanning to a well-distributed target group with no more client work to give, or (c)~DynamoDB's per-partition write ceiling is the new limit. A repeat experiment with 500--1{,}000 virtual users from multiple Locust hosts would be needed to distinguish among them. We also did not test asymmetric scaling (e.g., 1~API~:~4~workers).
\end{limitbox}

%==============================================================
\section*{Overall Evidence}
%==============================================================

\begin{systembox}
\textbf{Cross-cutting conclusion.} Across all four experiments the correctness invariant held without exception: the sum of balances was exactly \$1{,}010{,}000 (within a rounding tolerance of $10^{-12}$) after every run --- including the run whose worker was killed mid-commit, the runs with dozens of concurrent optimistic conflicts, and the runs that doubled and quadrupled the worker fleet. No workload or failure scenario produced a lost, created, or duplicated dollar.

\medskip
The performance results tell a consistent story: the system is queue-depth-bound on the worker side in the regime where the worker fleet is undersized, and partition-bound on the DynamoDB side once the workers catch up. Both are expected for a horizontally-scalable, queue-decoupled financial pipeline. All four experiments are reproducible end-to-end via the \texttt{make} targets \texttt{cloud-test-load-hot}, \texttt{cloud-crash-test}, \texttt{cloud-compare-locking}, and \texttt{scale-experiment}, each preflight-gated to refuse execution under any IAM principal other than the one the course allows.
\end{systembox}
